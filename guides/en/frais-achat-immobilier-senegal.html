What a Property Purchase in Senegal Really Costs, Beyond the Asking Price

Published on 2026-08-09

A buyer who has set aside exactly the amount shown in a listing is rarely at the end of their spending. The sale price covers only part of the outlay. Mandatory charges come on top, collected by the State and by the notary, and they fall due at signature rather than later. Discovering them at that point forces you to borrow in a hurry or walk away. Here is what they consist of.

Four items to budget for

Registration duties are a transfer tax paid to the revenue authorities so that the sale is officially recorded. This is usually the heaviest item after the price itself. The rate depends on the type of property and transaction, and it changes with each finance act. No figure found online replaces the rate that applies on the day of your deed: ask the notary or the tax office.

The notary's fees pay for the verification and drafting work. They follow a regulated scale based on the sale price, plus disbursements (sums the notary advances on your behalf). Any notary will give you that figure before signature if you ask.

Land registration costs cover entering your name at the Land Registry. Without that formality the sale exists between you and the seller but stays invisible to third parties, which leaves you exposed.

Surveying by a licensed surveyor is not always compulsory. It becomes close to essential on a plot whose boundaries are not physically marked. What it costs bears no comparison with a boundary dispute a few years later.

How to budget before committing

The reliable method fits in one sentence: ask the notary for a written estimate, based on the agreed price, before paying anything. Notaries prepare these routinely. The estimate will set out duties, fees, disbursements and registration costs, line by line.

Plan that envelope on top of the price, never inside it. A total budget of twenty million spent entirely on the sale price leaves nothing for the costs, and the transaction stalls at the last moment.

Costs on the seller's side

Sellers are not exempt. Depending on their situation and how long they have held the property, capital gains tax may apply, and certain tax documents will be required before the sale. A seller who discovers this at signature delays the whole transaction. If you are selling, raise the point with your notary as soon as you list.

Expensive mistakes

  • Paying before the notarized deed. Money handed over without a deed makes you the owner of nothing.
  • Under-declaring the price to reduce duties. The practice exists, it is illegal, and it rebounds on the buyer at resale: their apparent gain becomes enormous.
  • Forgetting land registration once the deed is signed, assuming everything is settled.
  • Confusing the deposit with the costs. A deposit counts towards the price; the costs come on top of it.

Frequently asked questions

Can notary fees be negotiated?
The fees follow a scale, so they cannot be freely negotiated. The sale price can be, and lowering it mechanically lowers the fees calculated on it.

Who pays, the buyer or the seller?
By custom the buyer bears the registration duties and the cost of the deed. That custom can be varied by agreement, provided it is written into the preliminary contract rather than agreed verbally.

Do these costs apply to land held under a deliberation?
The formalities differ from those for freehold title, and so does their cost. The land title status determines the procedure and therefore the bill. Check it before you build your budget.

This article describes the structure of the costs, not their amount: rates and scales change, and only a notary can give you the figure that applies to your transaction. It is not tax advice.
